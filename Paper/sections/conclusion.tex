\section{Conclusion}

This paper documents the firm-size wage premium in Colombia using harmonized
GEIH microdata for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. Real hourly income differs sharply
across firm-size categories, while employment remains concentrated in solo work
and micro-firms. Solo work and employment in firms with 2--10 workers together
account for about 69 percent of employment in 2008 and 61 percent in 2025.
Firms with 11--100 workers account for only 14 percent of employment in 2025.

Our preferred specification allows size premiums to differ by formality and
controls for sex, age, age squared, education, occupational position, and
sector-department-year fixed effects. Among informal workers, those in 101+
firms earn 37.2 percent more than observably comparable informal solo workers. Among formal workers,
the corresponding premium is 16.5 percent.
Both differences are statistically significant. Among informal workers, the
premium rises sharply across the smallest categories, but does not increase
monotonically through 101+. Among formal workers, 101+ employment pays more
than every smaller category. The formal premium is therefore smaller relative
to solo work, not absent.

The complementary specifications place these findings in context. A model
that constrains size premiums to be the same across formality groups shows
that observed worker characteristics and formality account for much of the
raw wage gap. Its dynamic estimates suggest widening between 2008 and 2025,
but the increase is not statistically significant. The approximate elasticity
exercise, also using simpler controls, produces estimates of 0.026 for formal
workers and 0.130 for informal workers. Thus, the stronger size association
among informal workers also appears in this approximate continuous-size
specification, although its controls differ from those of the preferred model.

The illustrative counterfactuals combine the preferred wage contrasts with
the 2025 distribution of workers and income. At unchanged hours and an
unchanged estimated wage structure, assigning informal workers the formal
pay prediction at their current size raises total predicted weekly labor income across all workers by
13.5 percent. Assignment to 101+ employment raises total income by 3.6 percent
when restricted to formal workers and by 7.7 percent when restricted to
informal workers. Assigning everyone to formal 101+ employment raises predicted
income by 25.4 percent. These magnitudes summarize conditional income
differences, not productivity gains. Their interpretation is especially
limited where few or no workers share the proposed combination of sector,
department, occupational position, size, and formality.

The results identify conditional wage differences associated with firm scale
within both formal and informal employment. They motivate investigating
barriers to firm growth and access to better-paid jobs, but do not measure
the productivity gains from reallocating workers. Those gains depend on
which workers move, the jobs they can access, and the response of wages and
production to the resulting changes in employment.
