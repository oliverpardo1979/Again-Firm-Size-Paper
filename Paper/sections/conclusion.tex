\section{Conclusion}

The firm-size wage premium in Colombia is not simply a formal-informal wage
gap. After controlling for observed worker characteristics, status in
employment, and sector-department-year conditions, workers in firms with more
than 100 workers earn more than solo workers of the same formality status.
In the full sample, the estimated premium is 37.0 percent among informal
workers and 17.2 percent among formal workers. The elasticity comparison also
places the formal estimate close to the observable-controls benchmark reported
by \citet{DiegmannMullerSchoefer2026}, while the informal estimate is about
2.7 times that benchmark.

Among employees, the estimated 101+/solo premiums are approximately
21 percent in both formality groups, although differences between their size
profiles remain at intermediate categories. The larger informal 101+/solo
premium in the full sample therefore does not generalize to salaried
employment alone. Explanations of the Colombian premium must account for the
distinction between salaried and non-salaried work, rather than treating the
full-sample estimates as differences in pay among employees alone.

Over time, the estimated informal 101+/solo premium increases between 2008
and 2025 in both samples, while the formal estimate changes relatively little.
The statistical evidence of informal widening is stronger in the full sample,
although it is sensitive to the clustering procedure and is not conclusive
among salaried workers. The results thus suggest a widening informal wage
gap, but do not establish a uniform increase across employment relationships
or a steady annual trend.

The income scenarios depend on both the conditional wage gaps and the
population to which they are applied. At unchanged hours and estimated wage
relationships, assigning everyone to formal employment in firms with more
than 100 workers raises total predicted weekly labor income by 25.9 percent
in the full sample and 12.3 percent in the salaried workers sample. Applying
the full-sample coefficients to salaried workers alone yields 13.0 percent,
close to the 12.3 percent obtained after re-estimation. The larger full-sample
scenario therefore depends substantially on including non-salaried workers
and their initial distribution across income, size, and formality categories.

These scenarios describe conditional income differences, not causal
productivity gains. Under the exercise's ceteris paribus assumptions,
and assuming that output per hour rises proportionately with predicted labor
income, the full-sample scenario would raise Colombia's productivity from
22.2 to 27.9 percent of the US level, using the \citet{OECD2026Productivity}
comparison as a benchmark. Even this extreme reassignment would leave nearly
all of the international productivity gap intact. Formalization and access
to larger firms may therefore matter substantially for individual incomes
without being sufficient to overcome Colombia's productivity shortfall. The
broader challenge includes raising productivity within firm-size and formality categories,
not only changing the distribution of workers across them.
