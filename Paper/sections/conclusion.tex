\section{Conclusion}

This paper documents the firm-size wage premium in Colombia using
GEIH microdata for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. Real hourly income differs sharply
across firm-size categories, while employment remains concentrated in solo work
and micro-firms. Solo work and employment in firms with 2--10 workers together
account for about 69 percent of employment in 2008 and 61 percent in 2025.
Firms with 11--100 workers account for only 14 percent of employment in 2025.

Our preferred specification allows size premiums to differ by formality and
controls for sex, age, age squared, education, occupational position, and
sector-department-year fixed effects. Among informal workers, those in 101+
firms earn 37.1 percent more than observably comparable informal solo workers. Among formal workers,
the corresponding premium is 16.5 percent.
The informal contrast remains significant across the alternative clustering
and wild bootstrap procedures. The formal contrast does not, with bootstrap
\(p\)-values of 0.184 under sector clustering and 0.188 under two-way
clustering. Among informal workers, the
premium rises sharply across the smallest categories, but does not increase
monotonically through 101+. Among formal workers, the 101+ advantage over
each category from 2--3 through 51--100 remains significant under all the
inference procedures examined. Evidence of a formal large-firm advantage
therefore survives, but it is not robust to using formal solo work as the
comparison group.

The salaried-only robustness preserves the formal 101+ advantage over
intermediate categories and the flattening of the informal profile above
small firms. However, its 101+/solo premiums are similar for formal and
informal workers, at 20.2 and 20.6 percent, and a direct test does not reject
equality. This similarity does not extend to the complete size profile.
The 11--19/solo premium remains much larger among informal employees,
at 24.7 percent, than among formal employees, at 6.5 percent. The stronger
informal 101+/solo premium in the full sample therefore does not generalize
to salaried employment alone, although differences at intermediate sizes remain.

The dynamic extension uses the same controls and allows all slopes to vary
by year. The informal 101+/solo premium rises from 35.6 percent in 2008 to
57.7 percent in 2025, while the formal premium changes from 12.6 to
13.5 percent. The formal endpoint change is not significant. The informal
change has \(p=0.011\) with sector-department clustering, but its bootstrap
\(p\)-values rise to 0.111 with sector clustering and 0.273 with two-way
clustering. The estimates suggest informal widening without statistically
robust evidence of an increase. Neither
endpoint comparison implies a steady annual trend.

With the preferred controls, the approximate firm-size elasticity is 0.040
among formal workers and 0.097 among informal workers. Both are positive
and significant under the alternative clustering and bootstrap procedures,
as is their difference. The formal estimate is
close in magnitude to the literature's observable-controls mean of 0.036,
while the informal estimate is about 2.7 times that benchmark. Thus, the
stronger size association among informal workers remains visible after
including occupational position and local sector-year conditions.

The illustrative counterfactuals combine the preferred wage contrasts with
the 2025 distribution of workers and income. At unchanged hours and an
unchanged estimated wage structure, assigning informal workers the formal
pay prediction at their current size raises total predicted weekly labor income across all workers by
13.5 percent. Assignment to 101+ employment raises total income by 3.6 percent
when restricted to formal workers and by 7.7 percent when restricted to
informal workers. Assigning everyone to formal 101+ employment raises predicted
income by 25.4 percent. These magnitudes summarize conditional income
differences, not productivity gains. Their interpretation is especially
limited where few or no workers share the proposed combination of sector,
department, occupational position, size, and formality.

The results identify conditional wage differences associated with firm scale
within both formal and informal employment. They motivate investigating
barriers to firm growth and access to better-paid jobs, but do not measure
the productivity gains from reallocating workers. Those gains depend on
which workers move, the jobs they can access, and the response of wages and
production to the resulting changes in employment.
