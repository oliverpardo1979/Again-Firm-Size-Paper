\section{Conclusion}

The firm-size wage premium in Colombia is not simply a formal-informal wage
gap, but neither can it be described by a single size profile within each
formality group. Once size coefficients vary by status in employment, the
101+/solo premium is about 16 percent for both formal and informal
private-sector employees. Among own-account workers, it is 54 percent for
formal workers and 46 percent for informal workers. The larger informal
premium in the common-profile benchmark therefore does not characterize
every employment relationship.

The distinction matters economically because reported firm size does not
describe the same relationship for every worker. For an employee, it describes
the employer. For an own-account contractor, it may describe the client
organization rather than the scale of a business the worker owns. The large
own-account premium cannot therefore be interpreted solely as the return to
expanding a small business. The dynamic estimates reinforce the need to
distinguish these groups. The estimated changes in the 101+/11--19 premium
among private-sector employees and in the 101+/solo premium among own-account
workers are not significant at 5 percent under the clustering procedures
examined.

The income scenarios combine these conditional differences with the
distribution of workers and income. Assigning everyone to formal 101+
employment within their original employment category raises total predicted
weekly income by 44.9 percent and mean predicted income among reassigned
workers by 76.6 percent. Own-account workers and employers account for most
of the total increase. Requiring at least 30 pooled observations in each
destination cell reduces the total increase to 26.1 percent by leaving more
workers at their original assignments. These are different reallocations,
not alternative estimates of the return to the same move.

These scenarios do not identify causal productivity gains. Under their
ceteris paribus assumptions, and the additional assumption that output per
hour rises proportionately with predicted labor income, the unrestricted
joint scenario would raise Colombia's productivity from 22.2 to 32.2 percent
of the US level, using \citet{OECD2026Productivity} as a benchmark. About
87 percent of the initial productivity gap would remain. Access to better-paid
employment relationships may matter substantially for individual incomes,
but reallocating workers across the observed categories is not sufficient
to account for Colombia's productivity shortfall.
