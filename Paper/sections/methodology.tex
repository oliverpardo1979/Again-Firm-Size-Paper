\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{From Simple to Preferred Specifications}
\label{subsec:preferred-specification}

We begin with a regression of log real hourly labor income on firm-size
indicators alone, with solo work as the reference category. We then add
sector-year fixed effects, worker characteristics, and formality in sequence.

Let \(i\), \(s\), and \(t\) index workers, sectors, and survey years.
The outcome \(w_{ist}\) is the real hourly income defined in the data section.
Write \(D_{ig}\) for the indicator of size category \(g\), and \(g_0\) for
solo work. The vector \(X_{ist}\) contains a female-worker indicator, age,
age squared, and education indicators. Unlike the four-group descriptive
comparisons, these indicators retain the six education categories and the
``does not know/not reported'' group, with lower secondary as the education
reference. The formality indicator \(F_{ist}\) equals one for pension
contributors and zero for noncontributors. With sector-year fixed effects
\(\lambda_{s\times t}\), the initial specification with all these controls is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-premium}
  \ln(w_{ist}) = \alpha
  + \sum_{g\neq g_0} b_g D_{ig}
  + X_{ist}'\gamma + \phi F_{ist}
  + \lambda_{s\times t}+\varepsilon_{ist}.
\end{equation}
All stages in this sequence use the same 5,268,514 complete observations
and GEIH expansion weights. Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions}
reports the sequence in the results section.
This sequence describes how the size coefficients change after accounting
for observed differences between workers and sectors. It does not decompose
the wage gap into causal contributions. Even after adding \(F\), the model
constrains each size coefficient \(b_g\) to be the same for formal and
informal workers.

We next retain this common-premium restriction while adding geographic
controls and occupational position. First, we add department-year fixed
effects to Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium}). We then replace the separate
sector-year and department-year effects with sector-department-year fixed
effects, before adding occupational-position fixed effects.
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness} reports these extensions.
The specifications with sector-department-year effects exclude 139 singleton
observations, leaving 5,268,375 observations.

Our preferred specification then allows each size coefficient to differ
between formal and informal workers by interacting the size indicators with
the formality indicator. Let \(d\) index departments and \(o(i)\) denote
worker \(i\)'s occupational position. Write \(\rho_{o(i)}\) for its fixed
effect and \(\lambda_{s\times d\times t}\) for the sector-department-year
fixed effect. The coefficient \(\beta_g\) is the informal log-wage gap
relative to informal solo work. The interaction coefficient \(\theta_g\)
is the difference between the formal and informal size gaps, so the formal
gap relative to formal solo work is \(\beta_g+\theta_g\). We normalize
\(\beta_{g_0}=\theta_{g_0}=0\), making \(\phi\) the formal-informal
log-wage gap among solo workers. The remaining variables retain their
definitions above. The preferred model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
  \ln(w_{isdt}) = \alpha + \phi F_{isdt}
  + \sum_{g\neq g_0}(\beta_g+\theta_g F_{isdt})D_{ig}
  + X_{isdt}'\gamma + \rho_{o(i)} + \lambda_{s\times d\times t}
  + \varepsilon_{isdt}.
\end{equation}
The size coefficients compare firm-size categories within sector-department-year
cells, separately by
formality and conditional on the included worker and occupational-position
controls. Controlling for occupational position
is relevant because solo work and employment in larger firms differ substantially
in the prevalence of own-account and wage employment. These controls describe
conditional comparisons, rather than identifying the total effect of moving
from own-account work to a salaried job.

We estimate the model with GEIH expansion weights. Column (4) of
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness} reports this
preferred specification. Columns (1)--(3) provide comparisons with progressively
more detailed geographic controls. The estimation sample for column (4)
contains 5,268,375 observations.

For any estimated log-wage contrast
\(\widehat{\Delta}\), we report the percentage gap
as \(100[\exp(\widehat{\Delta})-1]\). The underlying wage ratio is a ratio
of exponentiated conditional mean log incomes, not automatically a ratio of
arithmetic mean incomes as in the descriptive tables.

The model distinguishes three comparisons. For an origin size \(g\) and a
destination size \(h\), which may be larger or smaller, let
\(\Delta^F_{g\to h}\) and \(\Delta^I_{g\to h}\) denote the formal and
informal within-status log-wage differences. Let \(\Delta^{I\to F}_g\)
denote the formal-minus-informal log-wage difference at size \(g\). Then
\begin{align}
  \Delta^{F}_{g\to h} &= (\beta_h+\theta_h)-(\beta_g+\theta_g), \nonumber\\
  \Delta^{I}_{g\to h} &= \beta_h-\beta_g, \label{eq:preferred-contrasts}\\
  \Delta^{I\to F}_{g} &= \phi+\theta_g. \nonumber
\end{align}
For the third comparison, the percentage premium uses informal workers as
the denominator. Changing the omitted size category to 101+ or
11--19 workers leaves fitted log incomes unchanged. A given pairwise contrast
and its standard error are also unchanged, although the coefficients displayed
relative to the new base differ. These are adjusted wage comparisons. Interpreting them as the gains
from actual worker movements requires additional assumptions about selection
and the response of employers and wages, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
Section~\ref{subsec:income-counterfactuals} uses these contrasts in illustrative
income scenarios for the 2025 workforce, holding hours and the estimated
coefficients fixed.

\subsection{Inference and Comparison Specifications}
\label{subsec:inference}

We cluster standard errors by sector-department, allowing dependence across
workers and years within 559 clusters. As a sensitivity check, we also
cluster by sector, allowing dependence across departments but leaving only
17 clusters. The initial sequence in
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions} uses sector clustering.
The geographic and occupational-position extensions use sector-department
clustering.

Tests of individual coefficients and contrasts are two-sided. Their
\(p\)-values and 95 percent
confidence intervals use a Student t distribution with \(G-1\) degrees of
freedom, where \(G\) is the number of clusters. This gives 16 degrees of
freedom for sector clustering and 558 for the preferred model. The variance
of a contrast includes the covariances between its coefficients. For joint
hypotheses, we use Wald \(F\) tests.
Their numerator degrees of freedom equal the number of restrictions, and
their denominator degrees of freedom equal \(G-1\).

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:preferred-clustering-sensitivity} holds the estimation
sample fixed and separates changes in the controls from changes in clustering.

The results therefore proceed from a common size premium with sequential
controls to the preferred formality-specific estimates. The appendix reports
alternative reference categories and additional comparisons, including a
size-by-formality model with sector-year effects but without occupational-position
or department controls, defined in Equation~(\ref{eq:formality-interaction}).
Those supplementary estimates do not replace the preferred within-formality
comparisons.

\subsection{Changes over Time}

We extend Equation~(\ref{eq:main}) by allowing the firm-size premiums, the
formality coefficient, and all worker and occupational-position coefficients
to vary by year:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preferred-dynamics}
  \ln(w_{isdt}) = \phi_t F_{isdt}
  + \sum_{g\neq g_0}(\beta_{gt}+\theta_{gt}F_{isdt})D_{ig}
  + X_{isdt}'\gamma_t+\rho_{o(i),t}
  + \lambda_{s\times d\times t}+\varepsilon_{isdt}.
\end{equation}
The fixed effects absorb the intercepts. Estimating this model separately by
year gives the annual profiles. Each regression includes the preferred worker
and occupational-position controls and sector-department fixed effects.
The annual samples partition the preferred pooled sample. This extension
allows changes in other conditional wage differences rather than forcing
them into the estimated size premiums.

To test the change between 2008 and 2025, we estimate both years jointly,
interacting all non-fixed-effect regressors with an indicator equal to one
in 2025 and zero in 2008. The
point estimates reproduce the separate endpoint regressions. Joint estimation
also retains the covariance between the endpoint estimates within a
sector-department cluster. For informal workers, the null is
\(H_0:\beta_{g,2025}-\beta_{g,2008}=0\). For formal workers, it is
\(H_0:(\beta_{g,2025}+\theta_{g,2025})-(\beta_{g,2008}+\theta_{g,2008})=0\).
Each two-sided test concerns one size-formality comparison. These are
pointwise tests, not tests of all changes jointly, and they are not adjusted
for multiple comparisons. A positive difference means that the
conditional size-to-solo wage ratio increased. It does not establish a steady
annual rise or identify which group's wages changed.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:preferred-extension-clustering} checks how these
2008--2025 tests change under sector rather than sector-department clustering.

\subsection{Approximate Firm-Size Elasticities}

GEIH reports size categories rather than exact worker
counts. To obtain an approximate elasticity, we assign sizes of 1, 2.5,
4.5, 8, 15, 25, 40.5, 75.5, and 150 to the nine firm-size categories in
their stated order. Let \(\widetilde{Size}_{isdt}\) denote this assigned size.
We replace the size indicators with its logarithm and an interaction with
formality. Write \(\eta\) for the informal elasticity and \(\kappa\) for
the formal-minus-informal elasticity difference. The model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preferred-elasticity}
  \ln(w_{isdt})=\alpha+\phi F_{isdt}
  +(\eta+\kappa F_{isdt})\ln(\widetilde{Size}_{isdt})
  +X_{isdt}'\gamma+\rho_{o(i)}+\lambda_{s\times d\times t}
  +\varepsilon_{isdt}.
\end{equation}
The formal elasticity is \(\eta+\kappa\). Both elasticities come from a single interacted regression,
with the preferred sample, controls, weights, and clustering. The coefficient
\(\kappa\) tests whether the slopes differ between formality groups. We also
report a raw regression of log income on log assigned size alone and a
common-slope version imposing
\(\kappa=0\), with formality and the preferred controls included in the latter.

These slopes depend on the assigned sizes, especially the value
assigned to 101+. They also impose a constant slope within each formality
group, whereas Equation~(\ref{eq:main}) allows each category to differ.
We compare them with the literature means reported by
\citet{DiegmannMullerSchoefer2026}, recognizing differences in populations and
controls. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:preferred-extension-clustering} reports the
sensitivity of the elasticity tests to sector clustering. The appendix also
retains a version with fewer controls in Equation~(\ref{eq:elasticity}) for
comparison. We also estimate a quadratic extension, adding the square of log
assigned size and allowing both size coefficients to differ by formality.
Equation~(\ref{eq:polynomial-size-profile}) sets out that model. Its tests ask
whether a linear and a squared term per formality group can reproduce the
eight category-specific contrasts in Equation~(\ref{eq:main}), and check
sensitivity to the size assigned to 101+.
