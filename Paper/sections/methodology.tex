\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Estimating the Firm-Size Wage Premium}
\label{subsec:preferred-specification}

%We begin with a regression of log real hourly labor income on firm-size indicators alone, with solo work as the reference category. We then add sector-year fixed effects, worker characteristics, and formality in sequence.

Let \(i\), \(s\), and \(t\) index workers, sectors, and survey years.
The variable \(w_{ist}\) denotes real hourly income.
Write \(D_{ig}\) for the indicator of size category \(g\), and \(g_0\) for
solo work. The vector \(X_i\) contains a female-worker indicator, age,
age squared, and education indicators. The formality indicator \(F_i\) equals one for pension
contributors and zero for noncontributors. With sector-year fixed effects
\(\lambda_{s\times t}\), we estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-premium}
  \ln(w_{ist}) = \alpha
  + \sum_{g\neq g_0} \beta_g D_{ig}
  + X_i'\gamma + \phi F_i
  + \lambda_{s\times t}+\varepsilon_{ist}.
\end{equation}
%All stages in this sequence use the same 5,268,514 complete observations and GEIH expansion weights. This sequence describes how the size coefficients change after accounting for observed differences between workers and sectors. Even after adding \(F\), the model constrains each size coefficient \(\beta_g\) to be the same for formal and informal workers.

%We next retain this common-premium restriction while adding geographic controls and status in employment. First, we add department-year fixed effects to Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium}). We then replace the separate
%sector-year and department-year effects with sector-department-year fixed effects, before adding status-in-employment fixed effects.
Next, we add status-in-employment fixed effects \(\rho_o\) and replace
the sector-year effects with sector-department-year fixed effects
\(\lambda_{s\times d\times t}\). The indices \(o\) and \(d\) denote
status-in-employment categories and departments.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-premium-geography}
  \ln(w_{iosdt}) = \alpha
  + \sum_{g\neq g_0} \beta_g D_{ig}
  + X_i'\gamma + \phi F_i
  + \rho_o + \lambda_{s\times d\times t}
  + \varepsilon_{iosdt}.
\end{equation}
%The specifications with sector-department-year effects exclude 139 singleton observations, leaving 5,268,375 observations.

The formality-interacted benchmark, M0, allows each size coefficient to differ
between formal and informal workers by interacting the size indicators with
\(F_i\). For size \(g\), the log-wage gap relative to solo workers of the
same formality status is \(\beta_g\) for informal workers and
\(\beta_g+\theta_g\) for formal workers. We normalize
\(\beta_{g_0}=\theta_{g_0}=0\), making \(\phi\) the formal-informal
log-wage gap among solo workers.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
  \ln(w_{iosdt}) = \alpha + \phi F_i
  + \sum_{g\neq g_0}(\beta_g+\theta_g F_i)D_{ig}
  + X_i'\gamma + \rho_o + \lambda_{s\times d\times t}
  + \varepsilon_{iosdt}.
\end{equation}

Controlling for status in employment is relevant because solo work and employment in larger firms differ substantially in the prevalence of own-account and wage employment. %These controls describe conditional comparisons, rather than identifying the total effect of moving from own-account work to a salaried job.

M0 permits different size profiles by formality but constrains each profile
to be common across status-in-employment categories. We relax this restriction
in two steps. Let \(g(i)\) denote worker \(i\)'s size category.
Model M1 includes a separate size profile for each status in employment
and a status-specific formal-informal level difference:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:status-size}
  \ln(w_{iosdt}) = a_{o,g(i)}+\phi_o F_i+X_i'\gamma
  +\lambda_{s\times d\times t}+\varepsilon_{iosdt}.
\end{equation}
Thus, M1 compares sizes within the same employment relationship but imposes
the same size contrasts for its formal and informal workers. M0 and M1
relax different restrictions and are not nested.

Our most flexible specification, M2, allows every observed combination of
status in employment, firm size, and formality to have its own intercept:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:status-size-formality}
  \ln(w_{iosdt}) = a_{o,g(i),F_i}+X_i'\gamma
  +\lambda_{s\times d\times t}+\varepsilon_{iosdt}.
\end{equation}
M2 contains both M0 and M1 as restricted cases. All three models use the
same sample and retain common worker-control coefficients and
sector-department-year effects. They are estimated jointly across employment
categories, rather than separately for each category.
The cell-intercept notation includes the main effects and lower-order
interactions. One observed cell is normalized to zero. An empty cell has
no estimated intercept and cannot serve as a destination or reference.

In M2, the log-wage contrast between sizes \(g\) and \(h\) at status \(o\)
and formality \(f\) is \(a_{o,h,f}-a_{o,g,f}\). The formal-informal
contrast at a given size and status is \(a_{o,g,1}-a_{o,g,0}\).
We report size contrasts against solo work and against 11--19 workers,
using the latter where solo work is not a meaningful comparison.

For each estimated log-wage contrast \(\widehat{\Delta}\), we report the percentage gap
\(100[\exp(\widehat{\Delta})-1]\). This transformation compares
exponentiated conditional mean log incomes%, not necessarily arithmetic mean incomes as in the descriptive tables
.

Standard errors are clustered by sector for Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium})
and by sector-department for Equations~(\ref{eq:common-premium-geography})
and models M0--M2.
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness} also reports
Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium}) with sector-department clustering to
compare the geographic specifications under the same clustering convention.
The coefficients in its first column match Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions},
column (4), but the standard errors use the different grouping.
Reported \(p\)-values are unadjusted for multiple comparisons.
The appendix details the alternative clustering procedures.

\subsection{Changes over Time}

We extend M2 by allowing its cell intercepts and worker-control coefficients
to vary by year. Let \(\mathcal T\) denote the sample years.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preferred-dynamics}
  \ln(w_{iosdt}) =
  \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T}\mathbf{1}\{t=\tau\}
  \left[a_{o,g(i),F_i,\tau}+X_i'\gamma_\tau\right]
  +\lambda_{s\times d\times t}+\varepsilon_{iosdt}.
\end{equation}
Annual regressions describe the path of each size contrast within the same
employment and formality group. To test the change between 2008 and 2025,
we estimate the two years jointly, retaining cross-year covariance within
clusters. For sizes \(g\) and \(h\), the null is
\[
H_0:(a_{o,h,f,2025}-a_{o,g,f,2025})
    -(a_{o,h,f,2008}-a_{o,g,f,2008})=0.
\]
We do not estimate a time comparison when either endpoint cell is absent.

\subsection{Approximate Firm-Size Elasticities}

GEIH reports size categories rather than exact worker
counts. To obtain an approximate elasticity, we assign sizes of 1, 2.5,
4.5, 8, 15, 25, 40.5, 75.5, and 150 to the nine firm-size categories in
their stated order. Let \(\widetilde{Size}_{iosdt}\) denote this assigned size.
For comparison with the literature, we retain M0's restriction of common
size coefficients across employment categories and replace the size
indicators with its logarithm and an interaction with formality. Write \(\eta\) for the informal elasticity and \(\kappa\) for
the formal-minus-informal elasticity difference. The model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:preferred-elasticity}
  \ln(w_{iosdt})=\alpha+\phi F_i
  +(\eta+\kappa F_i)\ln(\widetilde{Size}_{iosdt})
  +X_i'\gamma+\rho_o+\lambda_{s\times d\times t}
  +\varepsilon_{iosdt}.
\end{equation}
The formal elasticity is \(\eta+\kappa\). Both elasticities come from a single interacted regression
with the same sample and controls as M0. We test
\(H_0:\kappa=0\) to assess whether the slopes differ between formality groups. We also
report a raw regression of log income on log assigned size alone and a
common-slope version imposing
\(\kappa=0\), with formality and the M0 controls included in the latter.

These slopes depend on the assigned sizes, especially the value
assigned to 101+. They also impose a constant slope within each formality
group, whereas Equation~(\ref{eq:main}) allows each category to differ.
We compare them with the literature means reported by
\citet{DiegmannMullerSchoefer2026}. The appendix
reports a version with fewer controls in Equation~(\ref{eq:elasticity}) for
comparison. We also estimate a quadratic extension, adding the square of log
assigned size and allowing both size coefficients to differ by formality.
Equation~(\ref{eq:polynomial-size-profile}) sets out that model. Its tests ask
whether a linear and a squared term per formality group can reproduce the
eight category-specific contrasts in Equation~(\ref{eq:main}), and check
sensitivity to the size assigned to 101+.

\subsection{Income Counterfactuals}
\label{subsec:income-counterfactual-method}

We ask how much predicted labor income would change if workers moved into formal
employment, into larger firms, or both.
The exercise combines the estimated wage differences with the distribution
of workers and their initial earnings to quantify changes for reassigned
workers and for the workforce as a whole.

We consider four separate scenarios for the 22.6 million workers represented
by the 2025 sample. First, informal workers become formal without changing
firm size. Second, formal workers move to firms with 101 or more workers
while remaining formal. Third, informal workers move to that size category
while remaining informal. Fourth, all workers are assigned to formal
employment in firms with 101 or more workers.

We implement these illustrative scenarios using the pooled M2
coefficients from Equation~(\ref{eq:status-size-formality}). All estimated coefficients and
fixed effects remain unchanged, as do worker characteristics, status in employment, sector, department, weekly hours, and survey weights.
In particular, keeping status in employment fixed means that a comparison for
an own-account worker does not predict earnings after becoming a salaried employee.

Write \(F_i^c\) and \(D_{ig}^c\) for worker \(i\)'s assignments in scenario
\(c\). Formalization at the same firm size sets \(F_i^c=1\) for informal
workers, with \(D_{ig}^c=D_{ig}\). Reallocation to 101+ employment sets
\(D_{i,101+}^c=1\) and all other size indicators to zero, with
\(F_i^c=F_i\). The joint scenario combines \(F_i^c=1\) with the same
101+ assignment for all workers. Workers outside the target group or
already at the destination retain their observed assignments.

Let \(\widehat{\ell}_i\) be fitted log hourly income from
Equation~(\ref{eq:status-size-formality}), including the fixed effects, for worker \(i\)'s
observed size and formality status. Replacing \(F_i\) and \(D_{ig}\) with
\(F_i^c\) and \(D_{ig}^c\) sets the destination size \(g^c(i)\) and formality, giving counterfactual fitted log hourly income
\begin{equation}\label{eq:income-counterfactual-prediction}
  \widehat{\ell}_i^c =
    \widehat a_{o,g^c(i),F_i^c}
    +X_i'\widehat\gamma+\widehat\lambda_{s\times d\times t}.
\end{equation}

Let \(\mathcal I_{2025}\) denote the 2025 sample. With expansion weight
\(a_i\) and weekly hours \(h_i\), the percentage change in total
predicted weekly labor income under scenario \(c\) is\footnote{We assume a
common retransformation factor across workers and destinations, which
cancels from the income ratio.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:income-counterfactual}
  G_c=100\left[
  \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{2025}}a_i h_i
        \exp(\widehat{\ell}_i^c)}
       {\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{2025}}a_i h_i
        \exp(\widehat{\ell}_i)}-1\right].
\end{equation}
%This percentage change weights each individual's percentage change by that individual's share of baseline income, including survey weights and hours. It is not the average percentage wage change across workers. The target-group calculation restricts both sums to workers initially in the relevant formality group, including those already at the destination. Restricting both sums to workers whose size or formality changes gives the result for reassigned workers. Because the workers and their weights remain the same before and after reassignment, this is also the percentage change in their mean predicted weekly income.
