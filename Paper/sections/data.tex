\section{Data}

The empirical analysis uses microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH), Colombia's nationally representative labor force survey produced by the National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE). The harmonized file covers 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We exclude 2020 because pandemic-related changes to collection and questionnaire coverage limit comparability. DANE introduced telephone interviews in urban areas and reduced the questionnaire in March and April \citep{DANE2020GEIH}. The positive large-firm premium documented below appears both before and after the excluded year, but we do not report a robustness exercise that includes 2020. The data are repeated cross-sections, not a panel following the same workers. The working sample contains 5,353,440 worker observations pooled across survey years. The comparison specifications use 5,268,514 observations with complete worker controls. The preferred specification uses 5,268,375 observations after excluding 139 observations in sector-department-year cells containing only one observation.

The sample is restricted to employed workers with positive labor income, valid weekly hours, and positive survey expansion factors. We convert reported monthly labor income and weekly hours to a common hourly basis using 12 months and 52 weeks per year. These are conversion factors, not observations of each worker's annual earnings or hours. We then express hourly labor income in constant 2025 Colombian pesos using the end-of-year Consumer Price Index (CPI):

\begin{equation}
  w_{it} =
  \frac{12 \times MonthlyIncome_{it}}{52 \times WeeklyHours_{it}}\times
  \frac{CPI_{2025}}{CPI_t}.    
\end{equation}
Here, \(i\) indexes workers and \(t\) indexes survey years.

Firm size is measured using the worker-reported firm-size variable in GEIH. The construction harmonizes the categories into the following ordered groups: Solo, 2--3, 4--5, 6--10, 11--19, 20--30, 31--50, 51--100, and 101+. Solo identifies respondents who report working alone. It is distinct from occupational position, which identifies whether they are own-account workers, employees, or employers. When the newer questionnaire separates 101--200 and 201+ workers, both categories are collapsed into 101+ to maintain comparability with earlier years. The data do not contain employer identifiers, so we cannot link respondents who work for the same firm.

The control variables are also harmonized across survey changes. The regressions distinguish six substantive education categories: no education, preschool, primary, lower secondary, upper secondary, and higher education. They also retain a separate ``does not know/not reported'' category, containing 437 observations in the comparison sample. The descriptive education comparisons omit this last category, combine the first two into ``preschool or less,'' and combine the two secondary categories into ``secondary.'' Formality is defined using pension contribution status: workers who contribute to pensions are classified as formal, while workers who do not contribute are classified as informal. This definition follows the social-protection approach used in Latin American labor-market measurement, which identifies informality through lack of access to social-security benefits such as pension or health insurance \citep{WorldBankLACInformality2026}. It is also the worker-level definition used by \citet{OteroCortesEtAl2025} in their study of labor and business informality in Colombia. This is a worker-level measure of social protection, not a measure of the firm's legal registration. Economic activity is harmonized across revisions of Colombia's industrial classification (CIIU) into broad sectors. The preferred regressions include one fixed effect for each sector-department-year combination, treating Bogot\'a as a department-equivalent Capital District.

Occupational position distinguishes private-sector employees, government
employees, domestic workers, own-account workers, employers, unpaid family
workers, unpaid workers in other households, day laborers, and other workers.
We harmonize the codes by survey framework. In the 2021--2025 data, codes 7
and 8 identify day laborers and other workers, respectively, whereas the older
coding assigns those groups to 8 and 9. The older code 7 identifies unpaid
workers in other households. We use the questionnaire categories rather than
carrying these numeric codes unchanged across frameworks
\citep{DANE2025GEIHQuestionnaire}. The salaried-only robustness retains
private-sector employees, government employees, domestic workers, and day
laborers, including both formal and informal workers. It therefore restricts
occupational position, not firm size or formality.
