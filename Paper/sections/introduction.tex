\section{Introduction}

Firm size is a well-established correlate of wages in labor economics \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, the wage difference between small and large firms motivates asking whether firm growth or worker reallocation could improve productivity. A first step is to establish whether this wage difference persists after accounting for differences in worker characteristics and formality across firm sizes. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than observably similar workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is associated with observable worker characteristics, whether the gap has increased or decreased over time, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

A recent policy diagnosis for Colombia argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. %This diagnosis is suggestive, but it does not show whether firm size remains associated with higher wages after accounting for worker characteristics and formality.
However, the observed link between larger employers and higher household income may reflect the fact that larger firms employ workers with different characteristics, or that larger firms are more likely to offer formal jobs.

We study these questions using microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We recode firm size into categories that are comparable across survey years. Throughout the paper, \emph{wages} refers to hourly labor income, including earnings from self-employment. The sample includes employees, own-account workers, employers, and other workers with positive main-job labor income. We regress log real hourly labor income on firm-size indicators and progressively add worker characteristics, formality, geographic fixed effects, and status in employment. We then allow the size profile to differ by status in employment and, in our most flexible specification, by both status in employment and formality. The status-specific specifications control for sex, age, age squared, education, and sector-department-year effects.

The results show why allowing employment relationships to have different size profiles matters. A benchmark with common size coefficients across employment categories yields a 101+/solo premium of 37.0 percent for informal workers and 17.2 percent for formal workers. Once the size coefficients also vary by status in employment, private-sector employees have premiums of 16.2 percent within formal employment and 16.4 percent within informal employment. For own-account workers, the corresponding premiums are 53.9 and 46.2 percent. Thus, the larger informal premium in the benchmark does not describe every employment category. The own-account premium exceeds the private-employee premium within both formality groups, while the formal-informal difference in the 101+/solo premium is not statistically significant within either category.

These similarities between formal and informal 101+/solo premiums do not extend to all size comparisons. Relative to 11--19 workers, formal private-sector employees in 101+ firms earn 13.1 percent more, whereas the informal estimate is 4.3 percent lower. The latter contrast is sensitive to clustering. Among own-account workers, 101+ estimates exceed the 11--19 estimates in both formality groups, although the informal contrast is not significant at 5 percent with sector clustering.

The time comparisons also differ across employment relationships. Relative to 11--19 workers, the private-employee premium changes from 12.6 to 11.8 percent among formal workers and from 2.3 to 4.6 percent among informal workers between 2008 and 2025. Among own-account workers, the 101+/solo premium rises from 40.1 to 47.0 percent for formal workers and from 35.9 to 49.5 percent for informal workers. None of these changes is significant at 5 percent under the clustering procedures examined. The estimates therefore do not establish a general widening of the informal premium once size profiles are allowed to differ by status in employment.

The literature offers competing explanations for a premium that survives observable controls. Larger firms may hire workers with higher unmeasured skills, or employers may pay differently even for similar workers \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010,BalkanTumen2016}. A premium within formality groups shows that the association between firm size and wages is not simply a formal-informal contrast. The remaining premium motivates investigating potential gains from firm growth or worker reallocation, but does not establish that either would raise workers' earnings or aggregate output.

We also use the most flexible estimates to construct illustrative income scenarios
for the 22.6 million workers represented by the 2025 sample. We consider
formalization at the current firm size, moves to 101+ firms within each
formality group, and the joint assignment of all workers to formal 101+
employment. Status in employment, worker characteristics, hours, and the
estimated wage relationships remain unchanged. The joint scenario reassigns
17.00 million workers, raising their mean predicted weekly income by
76.6 percent and total predicted weekly income across all workers by
44.9 percent. Own-account workers and employers account for most of that
increase. Requiring at least 30 pooled observations in each destination cell
reduces the joint increase to 26.1 percent by leaving unsupported moves
unimplemented. These calculations describe income under specified assignments,
not the effects of an observed reallocation.
