\section{Introduction}

Firm size is a well-established correlate of wages in labor economics \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, the wage difference between small and large firms motivates asking whether firm growth or worker reallocation could improve productivity. A first step is to establish whether this wage difference persists after accounting for differences in worker characteristics and formality across firm sizes. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than observably similar workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is associated with observable worker characteristics, whether the gap has increased or decreased over time, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

A recent policy diagnosis for Colombia argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. %This diagnosis is suggestive, but it does not show whether firm size remains associated with higher wages after accounting for worker characteristics and formality.
However, the observed link between larger employers and higher household income may reflect the fact that larger firms employ workers with different characteristics, or that larger firms are more likely to offer formal jobs.

We study these questions using microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We recode firm size into categories that are comparable across survey years. Throughout the paper, ``wages'' refers to hourly labor income, including earnings from self-employment. The full sample includes salaried workers, own-account workers, employers, and other workers with positive labor income. We repeat the analysis for a salaried workers sample comprising private-sector employees, government employees, domestic workers, and day laborers. We regress log real hourly labor income on firm-size indicators and progressively add worker characteristics, formality, geographic fixed effects, and status in employment. Our preferred specification interacts firm size with formality and controls for sex, age, age squared, education, status in employment, and sector-department-year fixed effects. %It compares workers within the same formality group and accounts for differences between own-account work, wage employment, and other employment relationships.

First, in the preferred specification for the full sample, informal workers in firms with 101 or more workers earn 37.0 percent more than observably comparable informal solo workers. %The premium rises sharply across the smallest categories, but does not increase monotonically through the largest category.
Second, formal workers in 101+ firms earn an estimated 17.2 percent more than observably comparable formal solo workers. For this 101+/solo comparison, the estimated firm-size wage premium is therefore smaller among formal workers. Third, restricting the sample to salaried workers yields similar estimated 101+/solo premiums for formal and informal workers, at 20.8 and 21.3 percent, respectively. The larger informal premium in the full sample therefore does not generalize to the salaried workers sample for this comparison. Formal and informal premiums nevertheless differ at intermediate sizes.

Fourth, in the full sample, the estimated informal 101+/solo premium rises from 33.2 percent in 2008 to 57.1 percent in 2025, while the formal estimate changes from 16.6 to 13.5 percent. In the salaried workers sample, the informal estimate rises from 22.9 to 39.0 percent and the formal estimate from 15.4 to 19.0 percent. The estimated informal increase thus appears in both samples, but only the full-sample increase is significant at 5 percent under our sector-department clustering. Among salaried workers, neither endpoint change is significant at 5 percent, and the informal increase is not statistically distinguishable from the formal increase.

In the full sample, the pooled informal 101+/solo premium remains significant under alternative clustering procedures. The formal 101+/solo contrast and the difference between the formal and informal 101+/solo premiums are not significant at 5 percent with sector or two-way clustering. Formal 101+ workers retain a significant advantage over formal workers in the categories from 2--3 through 51--100.

The literature offers competing explanations for a premium that survives observable controls. Larger firms may hire workers with higher unmeasured skills, or employers may pay differently even for similar workers \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010,BalkanTumen2016}. A premium within formality groups shows that the association between firm size and wages is not simply a formal-informal contrast. The remaining premium motivates investigating potential gains from firm growth or worker reallocation, but does not establish that either would raise workers' earnings or aggregate output.
