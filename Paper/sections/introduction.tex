\section{Introduction}

Firm size is a well-established correlate of wages in labor economics \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, the wage difference between small and large firms motivates asking whether firm growth or worker reallocation could improve earnings. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than observably similar workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is associated with observable worker characteristics, whether the gap has increased or decreased over time, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

Our motivation to measure the firm-size wage premium in Colombia is both empirical and policy-oriented. A recent policy diagnosis argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. %This diagnosis is suggestive, but it does not show whether firm size remains associated with higher wages after accounting for worker characteristics and formality. 
However, the observed link between larger employers and higher wages may reflect the fact that larger firms employ workers with different characteristics, or that larger firms are more likely to offer formal jobs. Documenting the wage gap within formality groups and after worker controls helps to isolate the effect of firm size on wages and therefore labor productivity.

We study these questions using harmonized microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. Throughout the paper, ``wages'' refers to hourly labor income, including earnings from self-employment. ``Solo workers'' are workers who report working alone, not necessarily own-account workers. We first estimate a common firm-size premium and progressively add worker characteristics, formality, geographic fixed effects, and occupational position. We then allow the premium to differ by formality. Our preferred specification interacts firm size with formality and controls for sex, age, age squared, education, occupational position, and sector-department-year fixed effects. It compares workers within the same formality group and accounts for differences between own-account work, wage employment, and other employment relationships. We extend the preferred model to study changes over time. Because unobserved worker characteristics may still be correlated with firm size, the estimates are conditional wage gaps rather than causal effects of firm size. 

That being said, the literature offers competing explanations for a premium that survives observable controls. Larger firms may hire workers with higher unmeasured skills, or employers may pay differently even for similar workers. The remaining premium does not by itself establish that reallocating workers would raise output \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010,BalkanTumen2016}. Still, if there were no differences in wages across different firm sizes after controlling for observables, or if all differences were explained by the formality status, then scaling up businesess without formalization would not increase productivity.

The evidence delivers five main results. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution. Solo work and employment in firms with 2--10 workers together account for about 69 percent of employment in 2008 and 61 percent in 2025. Second, raw wage gaps are large across firm-size categories, sectors, education groups, and formality status, while the wage gap by sex is small by comparison. Third, in the preferred specification, informal workers in firms with 101 or more workers earn 37.1 percent more than observably comparable informal solo workers. The premium rises sharply across the smallest categories, but does not increase monotonically through the largest category. Fourth, formal workers in 101+ firms earn 16.5 percent more than observably comparable formal solo workers and more than workers in every smaller formal category. The formal premium is therefore smaller relative to solo work, not absent. Restricting the sample to salaried workers makes the 101+/solo premiums similar across formality groups, at about 20 percent each. Fifth, using the preferred controls in each year, the informal 101+/solo premium rises from 35.6 percent in 2008 to 57.7 percent in 2025, while the formal premium changes from 12.6 to 13.5 percent. Under sector-department clustering, the informal endpoint increase is significant and the formal change is not. The significance of the informal increase and of the pooled formal 101+/solo premium is sensitive to clustering by sector, which we report alongside the main inference.

We also use the preferred estimates to construct illustrative income scenarios
for the 2025 workforce. Holding hours and the estimated wage structure fixed,
assigning informal workers the formal pay prediction at their current firm
size raises total predicted weekly labor income across all workers by 13.5 percent. Assigning workers to
101+ employment without changing their formality raises total income by
3.6 percent when applied to formal workers and by 7.7 percent when applied to
informal workers. These calculations combine conditional wage gaps with the
distribution of workers and income. They do not estimate changes in output
or the causal effects of these reallocations.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes the GEIH data, the harmonization of firm-size categories, and the construction of real hourly labor income. Section~3 presents the descriptive evidence on employment shares and wage gaps by firm size, formality, education, sex, sector, and year. Section~4 introduces the econometric specifications. Section~5 proceeds from the simple specifications to the preferred estimates, then examines salaried workers, changes over time, elasticities, and illustrative income counterfactuals. The appendix reports alternative reference categories, comparison models, and inference checks. Section~6 discusses interpretation, limitations, and policy implications. Section~7 concludes.
