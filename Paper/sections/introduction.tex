\section{Introduction}

Firm size is a well-established correlate of wages in labor economics \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, the wage difference between small and large firms motivates asking whether firm growth or worker reallocation could improve productivity. A first step is to establish whether this wage difference persists after accounting for differences in worker characteristics and formality across firm sizes. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than observably similar workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is associated with observable worker characteristics, whether the gap has increased or decreased over time, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

Our motivation to measure the firm-size wage premium in Colombia is both empirical and policy-oriented. A recent policy diagnosis argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. %This diagnosis is suggestive, but it does not show whether firm size remains associated with higher wages after accounting for worker characteristics and formality. 
However, the observed link between larger employers and higher wages may reflect the fact that larger firms employ workers with different characteristics, or that larger firms are more likely to offer formal jobs. Documenting wage gaps within formality groups and after controlling for worker characteristics helps establish whether firm size remains associated with wages beyond these observed differences.

We study these questions using microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We recode firm size into categories that are comparable across survey years. Throughout the paper, ``wages'' refers to hourly labor income, including earnings from self-employment. We regress log real hourly labor income on firm-size indicators and progressively add worker characteristics, formality, geographic fixed effects, and occupational position. Our preferred specification interacts firm size with formality and controls for sex, age, age squared, education, occupational position, and sector-department-year fixed effects. %It compares workers within the same formality group and accounts for differences between own-account work, wage employment, and other employment relationships. We extend the preferred model to study changes over time. 
Because unobserved worker characteristics may still be correlated with firm size, the estimates are conditional wage gaps rather than causal effects of firm size.

The literature offers competing explanations for a premium that survives observable controls. Larger firms may hire workers with higher unmeasured skills, or employers may pay differently even for similar workers \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010,BalkanTumen2016}. A premium within formality groups shows that the association between firm size and wages is not simply a formal-informal contrast. The remaining premium motivates investigating potential gains from firm growth or worker reallocation, but does not establish that either would raise workers' earnings or aggregate output.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes the GEIH data, how firm-size categories are made comparable across years, and the construction of real hourly labor income. Section~3 presents the descriptive evidence on employment shares and wage gaps by firm size, formality, education, sex, sector, and year. Section~4 introduces the econometric specifications. Section~5 proceeds from the simple specifications to the preferred estimates, then examines salaried workers, changes over time, comparisons with the literature, and illustrative income scenarios. Section~6 discusses interpretation, limitations, and policy implications. Section~7 concludes. The appendix reports supplementary tables, figures, and checks of the estimates.
