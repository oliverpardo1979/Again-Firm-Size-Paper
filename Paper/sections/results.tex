\section{Results}

\subsection{Preferred Firm-Size Premiums by Formality}
\label{subsec:preferred-results}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness} reports the
size-by-formality specifications. Column (4), our preferred model in
Equation~(\ref{eq:main}), includes worker characteristics, occupational-position
controls, and sector-department-year fixed effects. Columns (1)--(3) show how
the estimates change as geographic controls are added before controlling for
occupational position. All four columns allow the size coefficients to differ
between formal and informal workers and cluster standard errors by
sector-department cells.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_local_geography_formality_robustness_table}

Among informal workers, column (4) shows a large wage gap between solo work and
employment in larger units. The premium relative to informal solo workers is
12.0 percent in firms with 2--3 workers, 29.1 percent in firms with 4--5 workers,
and 38.3 percent in firms with 6--10 workers. It reaches 42.5--45.2 percent
across the 11--100 categories and is 37.2 percent in 101+ firms. All these
comparisons with informal solo work are statistically significant. The premium
therefore rises sharply across the smallest categories, but does not increase
monotonically through the largest category.

Among formal workers, the preferred estimates also reveal a large-firm
advantage. Formal workers in 101+ firms earn 16.5 percent more than observably comparable
formal solo workers, and this difference is statistically significant at the
1 percent level. The 51--100 category has an 8.9 percent premium over formal
solo work, also significant at the 1 percent level. The smaller categories
do not form a uniformly increasing sequence relative to solo work. In
particular, the 2--3 category is 6.9 percent below formal solo workers.
The estimates for 4--50 workers range from -2.2 to 6.2 percent and are not
statistically different from zero at the 5 percent level. The evidence of a
formal premium relative to solo work is strongest in the two largest categories.

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness-101-reference}
reports the same models with 101+ workers as the reference within each
formality group. In column (4), every smaller formal category is below formal
101+ workers, and all these differences are statistically significant.
Expressed with the smaller category as the denominator, the 101+ premium is
approximately 7.0 percent relative to 51--100 workers and 25.2 percent relative
to 2--3 workers. Among informal workers, by contrast, 101+ workers earn more
than solo workers and workers in firms with 2--5 workers, but not more than
every intermediate category.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_local_geography_formality_robustness_101_reference_table}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness-11-19-reference}
makes the intermediate comparison explicit. In column (4), formal 101+ workers
earn 0.113 log points, or approximately 12.0 percent, more than formal workers
in firms with 11--19 workers. Informal 101+ workers, however, earn 0.038 log
points, or approximately 3.7 percent, less than informal workers in that
reference category. Both contrasts are statistically significant at the
5 percent level. The same regression therefore implies different answers
depending on both formality and the sizes being compared. These are
alternative normalizations of each specification, not separate robustness
checks. In particular, column (4) in all three tables comes from the same
preferred regression.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_local_geography_formality_robustness_11_19_reference_table}

The contrast with the literature is most direct for the informal profile.
\citet{BalkanTumen2016} find a more pronounced firm-size wage gap among informal
than formal jobs in Turkey. Our preferred estimates show a similar contrast
when larger categories are compared with solo work. They also show a
statistically significant advantage of the largest formal employers over
smaller formal categories. This distinction complements evidence that firm
size and formality are closely related in Colombia
\citep{OteroCortesEtAl2025,DazaGamboa2013,GutierrezRodriguezLesmes2023} and the
Ecuadorian evidence that controlling for firm size can eliminate an apparent
formal-sector premium in wages net of taxes \citep{ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010}. The Colombian
within-formality size comparisons cannot be reduced to a contrast between
formal and informal employment.

\subsection{The Common-Premium Benchmark}
\label{subsec:common-benchmark}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions} reports the comparison
specifications leading to Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium}). These models
constrain the size premium to be the same for formal and informal workers.
The 101+ coefficient falls from 1.019 log points without controls to 0.819
with sector-year effects, 0.598 after adding sex, age, age squared, and
education, and 0.359 after adding formality. The final coefficient corresponds
to a 43.2 percent premium. It is a benchmark under the common-premium
restriction, rather than either of the preferred within-formality estimates.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_firm_size_table}

Adding these controls reduces the raw 101+-to-solo log-wage coefficient by
roughly two thirds.
This attenuation is consistent with the classic firm-size wage premium
literature \citep{BrownMedoff1989,troske1999evidence}. Studies of Peru, Kenya
and Ghana, and a broader set of developing economies also document sizeable
large-employer wage differences after accounting for worker characteristics
\citep{Schaffner1998,SoderbomTealWambugu2005,reed2019large}. Colombian
firm-survey evidence emphasizes firm characteristics in wage differentials
\citep{iregui2012diferenciales}. The separate estimates reported in
Section~\ref{subsec:preferred-results} show why this single common premium
does not describe the formal and informal comparisons equally well.

Figure~\ref{fig:firm-size-wage-premium} expresses column (4) of
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions} as percentage premiums.
The estimates are 20.7 percent for 2--3 workers, 36.0 percent for 4--5,
37.3 percent for 6--10, roughly 31--35 percent for intermediate sizes,
and 43.2 percent for 101+. All pointwise 95 percent confidence intervals
exclude zero. The figure summarizes
the common-premium benchmark and should not be read as the preferred
formality-specific profile.

  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig57_\figurelang.png}
  \caption{Common-premium benchmark relative to solo workers}
  \label{fig:firm-size-wage-premium}
\end{figure}

Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions-101-reference}
and \ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions-11-19-reference} change the
reference category of the same common-premium models. In column (4), the
101+ coefficient relative to 11--19 workers is 0.057 log points and is not
statistically significant. This differs from the preferred model's separate
formal and informal contrasts. Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness},
\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness-101-reference}, and
\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness-11-19-reference} add geographic
and occupational-position controls while retaining the common-premium
restriction. They show that a positive common size association survives these
controls, but they do not allow the formal and informal profiles to differ.

\subsection{Comparison with the Simpler Formality Model}
\label{subsec:formality-comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:formality-interaction-coefficients} reports the simpler
size-by-formality model in Equation~(\ref{eq:formality-interaction}), with
worker controls and sector-year effects, but no occupational-position or
department controls. Its informal size coefficients are positive and
significant throughout. The formal interaction terms are negative: each
size-to-solo log-wage gap is smaller among formal workers than among informal
workers. In this comparison model, none of the formal premiums relative to
formal solo workers differs from zero at the
5 percent level with sector-clustered standard errors.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_formality_interaction_coefficients_table}

Figure~\ref{fig:firm-size-formality-premium} presents these simpler estimates.
The informal premium is 21.7 percent in 2--3 firms, about 48--51 percent
across most 6--100 categories, and 40.0 percent in 101+ firms. Formal estimates
range from 2.1 to 7.0 percent for the smaller non-solo categories and reach
16.5 percent for 101+, but their intervals include zero. The formal 101+
point estimate is close to the preferred estimate, yet the uncertainty differs.
Both the controls and the clustering level differ across the specifications.
The insignificant formal 101+/solo contrast in this figure must therefore
not be substituted for the significant contrast in our preferred model.

With informal workers as the denominator, formal premiums are 68.8 percent
among solo workers, roughly 16--23 percent in most intermediate categories,

\subsection{Dynamic Results}
\label{subsec:dynamic-results}

The dynamic comparisons ask whether the firm-size wage gap widened or narrowed
over time. Widening means that wages in a given size category rose relative to
solo-worker wages, after the included controls. Narrowing means that this
conditional wage ratio fell. The ratio alone does not show whether wages rose
or fell in either group.
These exercises retain the sector-year comparison controls described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. They do not estimate the preferred model
separately by year.

Figure~\ref{fig:firm-size-wage-premium-over-time} reports
Equation~(\ref{eq:dynamic-benchmark}). The common size premiums are positive
throughout the period in all categories. The largest firms have the highest
point estimates in most years, although their intervals are wider.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig59_\figurelang.png}
  \caption{Common-premium benchmark over time}
  \label{fig:firm-size-wage-premium-over-time}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-premium-trend-test} tests for a linear trend in these
common premiums. All trend estimates are positive, implying 2008--2025
increases in size-to-solo wage ratios from 3.2 percent for 20--30 workers to
7.9 percent for 101+ workers. The two-sided tests do not reject zero trends
at 5 percent. Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:firm-size-premium-trend-test-101-reference} and
\ref{tab:firm-size-premium-trend-test-11-19-reference} report the same exercise
with alternative bases. Relative to 11--19 workers, the implied 101+ increase
is 3.0 percent and is not significant.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_firm_size_trend_test}

The literature does not imply a uniform time path. For the United States,
\citet{BloomGuvenenSmithSongVonWachter2018} document a declining large-firm
premium. \citet{reed2019large} find that the premium tends to decline with
national income and over time across developing economies.
\citet{ColonnelliTagWebbWolter2018} document a large-firm premium in Brazil,
Germany, Sweden, and the United Kingdom, but no common trajectory.
Our common-premium benchmark instead indicates persistence, with positive
but statistically inconclusive trend estimates. This temporal comparison is
distinct from the preferred pooled estimates by formality.

Figure~\ref{fig:adjusted-formality-endpoints} relaxes the common-premium
restriction in separate regressions for 2008 and 2025. Each regression
controls for sex, age, age squared, education, and sector. In 2025, formal
estimates below 101+ range from -3.7 to 0.6 percent relative to formal solo
work, and the 101+ estimate is 10.5 percent with an interval including zero.
The 2008 formal profile is similar. Informal estimates in 2025 range from
24.3 percent in 2--3 firms to 53.6 percent in 101+ firms. These endpoint
comparisons show persistence of the informal size gap under the simpler
controls. They describe differences across years, whereas the preferred
specification pools the available years with additional controls.

\subsection{Benchmarking the Firm-Size Wage Elasticity}
\label{subsec:elasticity-benchmark}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-elasticity-benchmark} compares the approximate
elasticities in Equation~(\ref{eq:elasticity}) with the no-controls and
observable-controls means reported by \citet{DiegmannMullerSchoefer2026}.
These are magnitude comparisons across different data and specifications,
not tests against a common population parameter. The Colombian elasticities
use assigned values for worker-reported size bins. Except for the raw
regression, they include the sector-year comparison controls. They do not
include the preferred model's department
and occupational-position controls.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_elasticity_benchmark_table}

The raw Colombian elasticity is 0.203, compared with the literature mean of
0.062 without worker controls. After worker controls, sector-year effects,
and formality, the common full-sample elasticity falls to 0.071, compared
with the observable-controls benchmark of 0.036. Separate regressions yield
0.026 among formal workers and 0.130 among informal workers. The latter is
more than three times the literature benchmark, whereas the formal estimate
is closer to it. This is consistent with a stronger informal size association
under the comparison controls. It is not a decomposition of the full-sample
elasticity into formal and informal contributions.

Table~\ref{tab:polynomial-size-profile} and
Figure~\ref{fig:polynomial-size-profile} report the quadratic approximation
in Equation~(\ref{eq:polynomial-size-profile}). Its raw 101+/solo premium is
175.8 percent. With the sector-year comparison controls, the common-premium
estimate falls to 41.0 percent, close to the 43.2 percent categorical
benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions}.
In the controlled full-sample regression, the quadratic term is negative and
significant, implying a concave fitted relationship in assigned log size.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_polynomial_size_profile_table}

In separate formal and informal regressions, the implied 101+/solo
premiums are 8.6 percent and 46.9 percent, respectively. The formal
contrast is not significant in this polynomial comparison. Its functional
form and controls differ from the preferred categorical model, in which
the formal 101+/solo gap is significant. The polynomial smooths over
category-specific differences and remains a supplementary shape check.

\subsection{Illustrative Income Counterfactuals}
\label{subsec:income-counterfactuals}

The preferred estimates describe wage differences between groups. We now ask
how much total predicted labor income would change if workers received the
pay associated with another firm-size or formality category. We apply the
pooled coefficients from column (4) of
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness} to the 341,282
observations in the 2025 estimation sample. Each scenario changes only firm
size, formality, or both. Worker characteristics, occupational position,
sector, department, weekly hours, and survey weights remain fixed. Workers
already in the destination category retain their baseline predictions.

Let \(\widehat{\ell}_i\) be fitted log hourly income from
Equation~(\ref{eq:main}), including the fixed effects. We construct baseline
hourly income as \(\widehat w_i=S\exp(\widehat{\ell}_i)\), where \(S\) is the
survey-weighted mean of exponentiated residuals in 2025. This factor accounts
for the fact that exponentiating a mean log income does not generally recover
mean income in levels. We assume the same factor across workers and
destinations. If scenario \(c\) changes worker \(i\)'s size-formality component
\(\phi F_i+\beta_{g(i)}+\theta_{g(i)}F_i\) by
\(\widehat\Delta_i^c\), counterfactual hourly income is
\(\widehat w_i^c=\widehat w_i\exp(\widehat\Delta_i^c)\).
For a change in size or formality alone, this difference is one of the
contrasts in Equation~(\ref{eq:preferred-contrasts}). For a joint change, we
subtract the origin component from the destination component directly. Let
\(\mathcal I_{2025}\) denote the 2025 estimation sample. With expansion weight
\(a_i\) and weekly hours \(h_i\), the reported change is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:income-counterfactual}
  G_c=100\left[
  \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{2025}}a_i h_i\widehat w_i
        \exp(\widehat\Delta_i^c)}
       {\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{2025}}a_i h_i\widehat w_i}-1\right].
\end{equation}
Thus, the outcome is total predicted weekly labor income. Its percentage
change weights each individual's percentage change by that individual's share
of baseline income, including survey weights and hours. It is not the average
percentage wage change across workers. The common factor \(S\)
cancels from this ratio. The target-group calculation restricts both sums to
workers initially in the relevant formality group, including those already
at the destination.

Table~\ref{tab:income-counterfactuals-2025} reports four scenarios. Assigning
informal workers the formal pay prediction at their current firm size raises
their group's predicted income by 41.9 percent and total predicted income by
13.5 percent. The aggregate change is smaller because informal workers account
for 32.2 percent of baseline predicted labor income, despite representing
55.1 percent of workers. Assigning all formal workers to formal 101+ employment
raises formal workers' predicted income by 5.4 percent and total income by
3.6 percent. This is smaller than the 16.5 percent formal 101+/solo premium
in part because 54.9 percent of formal workers are already in 101+ firms and
receive no increase. The remaining workers face different estimated gaps, and
their contributions depend on their shares of baseline income.

\input{Paper/sections/income_counterfactuals_2025_table}

Assigning informal workers to informal 101+ employment raises their group's
predicted income by 23.9 percent and total income by 7.7 percent. This scenario
does not raise every informal worker's predicted income. The estimated informal
profile is higher in several intermediate categories than in 101+, so workers
starting in those categories receive lower predictions. Finally, assigning
everyone to formal 101+ employment raises total predicted income by
25.4 percent. This combined scenario is calculated directly. The separate
percentages cannot simply be added: informal workers undergo both changes,
the corresponding wage ratios compound multiplicatively, and the estimated
size premium itself depends on formality.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:income-counterfactual-sensitivity} reports alternative
destinations and a calculation that starts from observed rather than predicted
income. Multiplying observed income by the estimated destination-to-origin
wage ratio preserves each worker's residual, the difference between observed
and fitted log income. This alternative gives similar aggregate changes. Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:income-counterfactual-support} examines how strongly the
scenarios extrapolate beyond observed destination groups. For example,
39.1 percent of intended movers to informal 101+ employment have no observed
counterpart with the same sector, department, and occupational position in
that destination in 2025. These scenarios quantify changes in predicted labor
income under a fixed estimated wage structure. They are not causal estimates
of reallocation or formalization, and they do not incorporate adjustments in
wages, employment, or production. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their
interpretation.
