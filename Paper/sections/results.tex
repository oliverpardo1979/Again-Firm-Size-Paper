\section{Results}

\subsection{Raw Premium and Conditional Premium}
\label{subsec:sequential-results}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions} begins with the simplest
specification and adds controls in successive columns. Column (4) corresponds
to Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium}). In column (1), which
contains only firm-size indicators, the 101+/solo coefficient is 1.028 log
points. Adding sector-year fixed effects lowers it to 0.832 in column (2).
Adding sex, age, age squared, and education lowers it to 0.613 in column (3),
and adding formality lowers it to 0.372 in column (4). The final coefficient
corresponds to a conditional premium of 45.1 percent. All four estimates are
statistically significant, and all four columns use the same sample.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_firm_size_table}

Observed worker characteristics, sector-year conditions, and formality account
for a substantial part of the raw association between size and pay. A positive
conditional gap remains. This pattern is consistent with evidence that worker
composition accounts for part, but not all, of size-related wage differences
\citep{BrownMedoff1989,troske1999evidence,Schaffner1998,
SoderbomTealWambugu2005,reed2019large,iregui2012diferenciales}.
Adding formality as a control allows a level difference between formal
and informal workers, but does not allow their firm-size premiums to differ.

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness} keeps that restriction
and adds geographic and status-in-employment controls. The 101+/solo
coefficient is 0.372 with sector-year effects, 0.352 after adding department-year
effects, and 0.337 with sector-department-year effects. The specification in
Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium-geography}) also controls for status in employment, giving a coefficient of 0.343 in
column (4), equivalent to a 41.0 percent premium. %Thus, more detailed controls do not eliminate the common-premium estimate, although it does not decline at every step.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_local_geography_robustness_table}
\FloatBarrier

The 101+/11--19 coefficient falls from 0.394 log points without controls to
0.071 with the controls in Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium-geography}).
The resulting conditional premium of approximately
7.3 percent is much smaller than the 41.0 percent premium relative to solo
workers. Thus, the large gap between the extremes coexists with smaller gaps
between 101+ and intermediate-size firms. Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions-101-reference}--\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness-11-19-reference}
report the full control sequences using 101+ and 11--19 workers as
alternative reference categories.

\subsection{Premium Within Formal and Informal Employment}
\label{subsec:preferred-results}

Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness} relaxes the
common-premium restriction by interacting each size indicator with formality.
Its four columns use the same control sequence as
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-robustness}. Column (4) is the M0 benchmark in Equation~(\ref{eq:main}).

\input{Paper/sections/regression_local_geography_formality_robustness_table}

In column (4), the premium relative to informal solo workers is
11.9 percent in firms with 2--3 workers, 29.3 percent in firms with 4--5 workers,
and 38.3 percent in firms with 6--10 workers. It reaches 42.5--45.5 percent
across the 11--100 categories and is 37.0 percent in 101+ firms. All these
comparisons with informal solo work are statistically significant. The premium
therefore rises sharply across the smallest categories, but does not increase
monotonically through the largest category.

Formal workers in 101+ firms earn an estimated
17.2 percent more than observably comparable formal solo workers. The 51--100
category has a 9.3 percent premium over formal solo work. Formal workers in
the 2--3 category earn 7.2 percent less than formal solo workers.
The estimates for 4--50 workers range from -2.2 to 6.6 percent.

Figure~\ref{fig:preferred-formality-premium} displays the within-formality
contrasts from Equation~(\ref{eq:main}).
The informal estimates flatten across intermediate categories and fall at
101+, whereas the formal 101+ estimate is the largest in its panel.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:preferred-clustering-sensitivity} compares the
specifications in columns (1), (3), and (4) of
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness} on a common
sample under both clustering conventions. The formal 101+/solo estimate
is 18.2 percent with sector-year effects and 17.2 percent with the M0
controls. The underlying estimated log-wage contrast,
\(\widehat\beta_{101+}+\widehat\theta_{101+}\), has a standard error of 0.037 log points
under sector-department clustering, compared with 0.090 under sector
clustering. The corresponding two-sided \(p\)-values are below 0.001 and
0.096. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:preferred-clustering-comparison} extends this
check to department and two-way clustering. The formal 101+/solo contrast
is also insignificant at 5 percent with two-way clustering (\(p=0.074\)),
but remains significant with department clustering. All eight informal
size-to-solo contrasts remain significant under every clustering procedure.

With 101+ workers as the reference, column (4) of Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness-101-reference}
shows that every smaller formal
category has a lower point estimate than formal 101+ workers.
All seven comparisons with formal
categories from 2--3 through 51--100 remain significant at 5 percent under
the alternative clustering procedures, unlike the solo comparison.
Expressed with the smaller category as the denominator, the 101+ premium is
approximately 7.2 percent relative to 51--100 workers and 26.3 percent relative
to 2--3 workers. Among informal workers, by contrast, the point estimates in the same column
put 101+ workers above solo workers and workers in firms with 2--5 workers, but not above
every intermediate category.

Column (4) of Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-local-geography-formality-robustness-11-19-reference}
uses 11--19 workers as the reference. Formal 101+ workers
earn 0.118 log points, or approximately 12.5 percent, more than formal workers
in firms with 11--19 workers. Informal 101+ workers, however, earn 0.040 log
points, or approximately 3.9 percent, less than informal workers in that
reference category. The formal contrast remains significant with two-way
clustering (\(p<0.001\)), whereas the informal contrast does not
(\(p=0.151\)), despite its significance under sector-department clustering.
Changing the
reference category leaves fitted log incomes and any given pairwise contrast
and its standard error unchanged.

Table~\ref{tab:preferred-formality-gap} reports formal-informal wage gaps
from Equation~(\ref{eq:main}). Formal workers earn 59.6 percent more
than informal workers within solo work, 32.4 percent more in 2--3 firms,
and 36.6 percent more in 101+ firms. Across the 4--100 categories, the gaps
range from 15.8 to 20.7 percent. All nine gaps remain significant under
each alternative clustering procedure. These comparisons hold firm size fixed, whereas
Figure~\ref{fig:preferred-formality-premium} holds formality fixed.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_preferred_formality_gap_table}

\citet{BalkanTumen2016} find a more pronounced firm-size wage gap among informal
than formal jobs in Turkey. Their largest-to-smallest comparison is 500+ versus
1--9 workers, so the comparison with our 101+/solo estimates is qualitative.
The M0 estimates show a similar contrast
when larger categories are compared with solo work. They also show a
statistically significant wage advantage for formal workers in 101+ firms
over formal workers in the other non-solo size categories. This distinction complements evidence that firm
size and formality are closely related in Colombia
\citep{OteroCortesEtAl2025,DazaGamboa2013,GutierrezRodriguezLesmes2023} and the
Ecuadorian evidence that controlling for firm size can eliminate an apparent
formal-sector premium in wages net of taxes \citep{ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010}.

estimates from Equation~(\ref{eq:common-premium}), reported in column (4) of
Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-wage-premium-regressions}.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:formality-interaction-coefficients} and

model in Equation~(\ref{eq:formality-interaction}), with sector-year controls.
Table~\ref{tab:formal-101-contrasts} reports its

alternative normalizations.

\subsection{Benchmarking the Firm-Size Wage Elasticity}
\label{subsec:elasticity-benchmark}

Table~\ref{tab:preferred-elasticities} compares the approximate elasticities
from Equation~(\ref{eq:preferred-elasticity}) and its raw and common-slope
variants with the literature means without worker-heterogeneity controls and
with observable worker controls reported by \citet{DiegmannMullerSchoefer2026}.
The raw Colombian elasticity is 0.205, compared with their mean of 0.062
without worker-heterogeneity controls. After worker characteristics, status in employment,
sector-department-year effects, and formality are added, a model imposing
a common slope gives 0.060, compared with the literature mean of 0.036.

\input{Paper/sections/regression_preferred_elasticity_table}

Under M0's restriction of common size coefficients across employment categories,
allowing the slope to differ by formality gives an elasticity of 0.041 for
formal workers and 0.097 for informal workers. The formal estimate is close
in magnitude to the literature's observable-controls mean, whereas the informal
estimate is about 2.7 times that benchmark. The informal slope exceeds the
formal slope by \(-\widehat{\kappa}=0.055\), a statistically significant difference
(\(p<0.001\) with two-way clustering).
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:preferred-clustering-comparison} shows that both
slopes and their difference remain significant under all the clustering
procedures examined.

The literature comparison concerns magnitudes across different populations,
size measures, and controls, not tests against a common population parameter.
The assigned-size slopes also summarize the entire size distribution.
A positive informal slope does not contradict the lower categorical estimate
for informal 101+ workers than for several intermediate categories.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:firm-size-elasticity-benchmark} reports the simpler
elasticities of 0.029 for formal workers and 0.132 for informal workers.
They come from separate-formality regressions based on Equation~(\ref{eq:elasticity}),
with sector-year controls and no status-in-employment controls.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:polynomial-size-profile} reports the quadratic model in
Equation~(\ref{eq:polynomial-size-profile}), which uses the M0 controls.
Figure~\ref{fig:polynomial-size-profile} overlays its curves on the categorical
estimates from Equation~(\ref{eq:main}).
The tests in Table~\ref{tab:quadratic-preferred-sensitivity} reject the quadratic restriction on
Equation~(\ref{eq:main}) within
both formality groups, including under alternative assignments for 101+.
The quadratic is therefore a descriptive approximation, not a replacement
for M0's categorical estimates.
