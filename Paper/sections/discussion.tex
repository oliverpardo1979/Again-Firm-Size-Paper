\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The preferred results identify wage differences within both formal and informal
employment after accounting for occupational position and local sector-year
conditions. The premium relative to solo workers is larger among informal
workers, but formal employment also displays a statistically significant
large-firm advantage. These results rule out describing the formal size premium
as absent. They also show why the origin and destination categories matter.
Among formal workers, the 101+ category pays more than every smaller category.
Among informal workers, the largest gaps separate solo work and the smallest
units from larger employers, rather than every size category from the next.

The minimum-wage distributions in
Figure~\ref{fig:minwage-distribution-formality-size} suggest one possible reason
for the smaller wage differences across intermediate formal categories.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:minimum-wage-monthly-bunching-2025} shows that
39.1--46.3 percent of formal workers in firms with 4--100 workers earn within
10 percent of the monthly minimum wage. The corresponding shares are
17.8 percent for formal solo workers and 29.2 percent for formal workers in
101+ firms. This concentration is consistent with compression of formal
wages around the minimum, but it does not explain the conditional premiums by
itself. The preferred estimates still show differences between intermediate
formal categories and 101+ workers after controlling for occupational position.
Neither the kernel densities nor the regressions identify the causal effect
of the minimum wage.

A wage premium that survives observable controls does not identify a single
mechanism. As \citet{BrownMedoff1989} and \citet{OiIdson1999} emphasize, the
remaining gap may reflect unobserved worker quality, firm productivity, rent
sharing, efficiency wages, or match quality. The developing-country evidence
cautions against reducing the premium to sorting alone.
\citet{SoderbomTealWambugu2005} find substantial firm-size effects in Kenya and
Ghana after controlling for individual fixed effects. Matched employer-employee
studies also find an employer-associated component of wages
\citep{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,Song2019,
AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018,GerardLagosSeverniniCard2021,EngbomMoser2022}.
Recent German evidence adds a distinction between firm and establishment size.
\citet{DiegmannMullerSchoefer2026} find that the establishment-size wage
relationship disappears within multi-establishment firms, which points to
employer-wide attributes rather than establishment size alone. GEIH does not
identify employers or follow workers across firms, so the additional controls
in our preferred specification cannot separate these explanations.

The evidence is also not a direct test of misallocation. The link with
allocative efficiency comes from the marginal-product condition. In a
frictionless competitive benchmark with comparable labor inputs, efficient
allocation equalizes the value of the marginal product of labor across uses.
Wages then reflect that value, making persistent wage differences informative
about possible allocative frictions. If larger firms pay more for workers with
the same observed characteristics, this may indicate that labor is more
valuable there or that barriers prevent workers from reaching
higher-productivity matches. The evidence is a marker, not proof, because
unobserved worker quality and differences in wage setting can also produce
these gaps. Structural misallocation exercises use production assumptions and
information on inputs and output to estimate marginal products and quantify
the consequences of reallocation \citep{hsieh2009misallocation}.

For Colombia, \citet{PulidoGonzalez2024}, as presented in Section 3.3 and
Annex 4 of \citet{OteroCortesEtAl2025}, use a sector-choice model in general
equilibrium. They remove sector-specific distortions for all informal workers,
rather than assign an arbitrary share to new jobs. Workers' choices and
equilibrium wages determine how many change sectors. The implied minimum
reallocation is 35 percent of informal unskilled workers and 41 percent of
informal skilled workers, with an aggregate productivity gain of 4.4 percent.
A separate exercise raises informal workers' average productivity to the formal
benchmark, yielding 15.4 percent without sectoral reallocation. Neither
exercise changes workers' formal-informal group membership, and the model does
not distinguish firm-size categories.

Our preferred model instead provides the three conditional wage contrasts in
Equation~(\ref{eq:preferred-contrasts}): larger versus smaller formal employers,
larger versus smaller informal employers, and formal versus informal employment
at a given size. A wage-based simulation could apply these contrasts to
explicitly defined groups, holding their characteristics fixed. It would not
determine how many workers can actually move or how wages, employment, and
output respond when they do. In particular, keeping occupational position fixed
means that a comparison for own-account workers is not a prediction of their
earnings after becoming salaried employees. Such a simulation would require
checking that comparable workers are observed in both origin and destination
groups. It should be reported as a conditional earnings scenario, not as an
estimate of the aggregate productivity loss from misallocation.

The policy implication is therefore not that policy should favor large firms
as such or push workers mechanically into large firms. The relevant objective
is to reduce barriers that keep productive firms small, informal, or disconnected
from larger markets, while improving workers' access to better firm matches.
The wage differences within informal employment support particular attention
to productive solo and micro-units. Support for their growth should be designed
as a path toward formalization, not as a subsidy for remaining informal that
weakens incentives for formal firms to remain formal. The preferred results
also support examining barriers to growth and worker movement within formal
employment, given the significant advantage of 101+ firms over smaller formal
categories. The positive formal-informal wage gaps in the simpler comparison
specification motivate a separate question about access to formal jobs.
However, pension contribution measures worker formality, not firms' legal
registration, and the wage gaps do not establish that changing registration
alone raises productivity. The evidence identifies comparisons worth
investigating, but it does not rank the aggregate gains from formalization
against those from firm growth.
