\begin{frame}{The Question}
  \idea{The paper asks whether Colombian workers earn more in larger firms, and what remains of that gap after accounting for worker composition.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item How much higher is real hourly pay in large firms relative to solo work and micro-firms?
    \item How much of the gap is explained by sector, education, age, sex, and formality?
    \item Did the gap widen or narrow between 2008 and 2025?
    \item Does the size-wage relationship differ between formal and informal employment?
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Argument in One Slide}
  \begin{columns}[T,totalwidth=\textwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \idea{Empirical fact}
      \vspace{0.25cm}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Real hourly pay rises with firm size.
        \item The relationship survives controls for observable characteristics.
        \item The premium is much stronger within informal employment.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
      \idea{Interpretation}
      \vspace{0.25cm}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item The premium does not prove causality or misallocation by itself.
        \item It is a diagnostic magnitude for wage inequality, segmentation, and possible frictions.
        \item The policy discussion should focus on formalization and the growth of productive informal units, not on firm size alone.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\sectionframe{1. Motivation and Data}

\begin{frame}{Why Firm Size Matters}
  \idea{Firm size captures a visible dimension of the productive structure.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item In Colombia, a large share of employment is concentrated in own-account work and micro-firms.
    \item If hourly pay is systematically higher in larger firms, the firm-size distribution matters for wage inequality.
    \item The relationship can also be informative about frictions: barriers to growth, formalization, market access, or worker reallocation toward better firms.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Central Caution}
  \idea{A conditional wage premium is not a causal effect of firm size.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The conditional premium means that observationally similar workers earn more in larger firms.
    \item It does not prove that moving the same worker to a larger firm would raise aggregate output.
    \item The remaining gap may reflect unobserved selection, match quality, rents, firm wage policies, or productivity.
    \item We therefore interpret the premium as a diagnostic statistic, not as direct proof of efficiency or inefficiency.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Data}
  \idea{We use GEIH because it covers the broader labor market, not only formal firms.}

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Source: Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares, DANE.
    \item Period: 2008--2019 and 2021--2025.
    \item We exclude 2020 because the pandemic disrupted labor-market outcomes and survey collection.
    \item Working sample: 5.24 million worker-year observations with valid hourly labor income and positive expansion weights.
    \item Regression sample: 5.15 million observations with complete controls.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Main Variables}
  \begin{columns}[T,totalwidth=\textwidth]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \idea{Hourly pay}
      \vspace{0.25cm}
      \[
        w_{it} =
        \frac{12 \times MonthlyIncome_{it}}
        {52 \times WeeklyHours_{it}}
        \times
        \frac{CPI_{2025}}{CPI_t}
      \]
      \vspace{0.1cm}
      {\small Real hourly labor income in constant 2025 Colombian pesos.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \idea{Firm size}
      \vspace{0.25cm}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Solo
        \item 2--3, 4--5, 6--10
        \item 11--19, 20--30, 31--50, 51--100
        \item 101+
      \end{itemize}
      {\small The 101+ category harmonizes questionnaire changes.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Formality}
  \idea{We define formality through pension contribution.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A worker is formal if the worker contributes to pensions.
    \item A worker is informal if the worker does not contribute to pensions.
    \item The definition captures access to contributory social security.
    \item The definition does not use firm size, which is important because firm size is the variable we want to study.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\sectionframe{3. Conditional Firm-Size Wage Premium}

\begin{frame}{Main Specification}
  \idea{We estimate conditional wage gaps, not causal effects.}

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  \[
    \ln(w_{ist}) =
    \alpha
    + \sum_{g \neq Solo} \beta_g \mathbf{1}\{Size_{ist}=g\}
    + X_{ist}'\gamma
    + \lambda_{s \times t}
    + \varepsilon_{ist}
  \]

  \vspace{0.25cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \(w_{ist}\): real hourly labor income.
    \item \(X_{ist}\): sex, age, age squared, education, and formality.
    \item \(\lambda_{s \times t}\): sector-year fixed effects.
    \item Coefficients are transformed as \(100 \times [\exp(\hat{\beta}_g)-1]\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Happens When We Add Controls}
  \idea{The 101+ premium falls substantially, but it does not disappear.}

  \vspace{0.25cm}
  \begin{center}
  \scriptsize

    Specification & 101+ coefficient & Interpretation \\

    No controls & 1.019 & Very large raw gap \\

\end{frame}

\sectionframe{4. Evolution over Time}

\begin{frame}{What It Means for the Premium to Steepen or Flatten}
  \idea{The evolution of the premium asks whether firm size became more or less strongly associated with wages.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A steeper premium means that wage differences by firm size increased.
    \item A flatter premium means that wage differences by firm size declined.
    \item The test does not identify whether the change came from faster wage growth at the bottom, slower wage growth at the top, or changes in composition.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\sectionframe{5. Formality and Nonlinearities}

\begin{frame}{Why Split Formal and Informal Workers}
  \idea{Formality can both mediate and modify the firm-size premium.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item It can mediate the premium because large firms are more likely to offer formal jobs.
    \item It can modify the premium because the size-wage relationship may differ within formal and informal employment.
    \item If firm size matters mainly within informal employment, the policy interpretation changes.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Specification with Firm Size by Formality}
  \[
    \ln(w_{ist}) =
    \alpha + \phi Formal_{ist}
    + \sum_{g \neq Solo}(\beta_g + \theta_g Formal_{ist})
      \mathbf{1}\{Size_{ist}=g\}
    + X_{ist}'\gamma
    + \lambda_{s \times t}
    + \varepsilon_{ist}
  \]

  \vspace{0.25cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \(\beta_g\): firm-size premium among informal workers.
    \item \(\beta_g+\theta_g\): firm-size premium among formal workers.
    \item \(\phi+\theta_g\): formal-informal wage gap within size group \(g\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\sectionframe{6. Elasticity and Literature Benchmark}

\begin{frame}{Why Estimate an Approximate Elasticity}
  \idea{The paper uses firm-size categories, but much of the literature reports wage-size elasticities.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item GEIH reports firm size in intervals.
    \item We assign representative values to each interval: 1, 2.5, 4.5, 8, 15, 25, 40.5, 75.5, and 150.
    \item The elasticity is only a benchmarking exercise.
    \item The categorical estimates remain the main empirical results.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{International Benchmark}
  \idea{Colombia's conditional elasticity is high relative to the literature, and the difference mainly comes from informal employment.}

  \vspace{0.25cm}

\sectionframe{7. Interpretation and Implications}

\begin{frame}{What We Know}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Real hourly income rises with firm size.
    \item The premium falls substantially with controls, but remains positive.
    \item There is no statistically conclusive evidence of secular decline.
    \item The conditional premium is especially strong within informal employment.
    \item Formality raises wage levels within every firm-size category.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What These Data Cannot Tell Us}
  \idea{GEIH does not identify firms or observe output, capital, or marginal products.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item We cannot fully separate unobserved selection from firm-side differences.
    \item We cannot estimate employer fixed effects.
    \item We cannot directly measure marginal products.
    \item This is why we talk about markers of frictions, not direct proof of misallocation.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Policy Implication}
  \idea{The conclusion is not to favor large firms because they are large.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The relevant objective is to reduce barriers that keep productive units small, informal, or disconnected from markets.
    \item Because formality raises wages within each firm-size category, moving jobs toward formality remains a policy priority.
    \item Because the firm-size premium is strongest among informal workers, it also matters that productive informal units can grow, organize, and connect to markets.
    \item This support should be a path toward formalization, not a subsidy for remaining informal.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Bad Policy Reading}
  \idea{Subsidies that preserve small scale may reinforce the structure documented in the paper.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Supporting small firms is not enough if support does not affect productivity, formalization, or market access.
    \item Policy should avoid regulatory thresholds that make growth costly.
    \item Policy should also avoid incentives for formal firms to become informal in order to receive benefits.
    \item The focus should be productivity, formalization, management, technology, and access to larger customers.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
  \idea{Firm size is closely associated with real hourly pay in Colombia.}

  \vspace{0.35cm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The 101+ premium relative to solo workers is large in raw averages and remains 43.2\% after controls.
    \item The pattern is not only a formal-informal difference.
    \item The strongest premium is within informal employment.
    \item The policy question is not how to move workers mechanically into large firms, but why so many workers remain in small, informal, low-paying units.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Questions for Discussion}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item How much of the conditional premium reflects unobserved selection, and how much reflects firm-side differences?
    \item Which policies help productive informal units grow without subsidizing informality?
    \item What data would allow us to move from a diagnostic statistic to a more direct test of allocative efficiency?
    \item How should this evidence be connected to productivity, education, regulation, and market access?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
